The ground shakes for fifteen seconds, and a city of a million people is gone. A river breaks its banks, and an area bigger than England disappears under water. A mountain explodes, and the whole planet loses its summer.

These are the ten deadliest natural disasters in recorded history. Between them, they've taken more lives than most wars.

Some of them lasted seconds. One of them made a sound so loud it was heard nearly 5,000 kilometres away. Another changed the weather of the whole planet for a year. And the worst one on this list killed more people than any other natural disaster ever recorded, and most people have never even heard of it.

We're counting down from number ten to number one, ranked by how many lives were lost. Death tolls from long ago are hard to pin down, so for each one I'll give you the range historians actually argue about. Let's start.

Number 10. Krakatoa, Indonesia, 1883.

In August 1883, a volcanic island between Java and Sumatra tore itself apart. The final explosions were so powerful that they made the loudest sound in recorded history. People on an island near Africa, nearly 5,000 kilometres away, thought they were hearing distant gunfire.

But the explosion itself wasn't the main killer. When the volcano collapsed, it pushed out huge tsunamis, some more than 30 metres high, that smashed into the coasts nearby. Around 36,000 people died.

For months afterwards, the ash in the sky turned sunsets around the world blood red. Some people even think the burning sky in the famous painting The Scream was inspired by those sunsets.

Number 9. Mount Tambora, Indonesia, 1815.

Just 68 years earlier, an even bigger volcano erupted in the same country. Tambora is the largest volcanic eruption in recorded history. It blew so much rock into the air that the mountain lost more than a kilometre of its height.

Around 10,000 people died in the eruption itself. But that was only the beginning. Tens of thousands more on the nearby islands died afterwards from starvation and disease, because the ash had destroyed their crops.

And then it went global. The ash in the upper sky dimmed the sun, and 1816 became known as the Year Without a Summer. It snowed in June in parts of America. Crops failed across Europe. And in Switzerland, a group of friends were stuck indoors because of the terrible weather, so they had a competition to write scary stories. One of them, a young woman named Mary Shelley, came up with Frankenstein.

Number 8. The Haiyuan earthquake, China, 1920.

On a December evening in 1920, the ground in northwest China started to shake, and it didn't stop for minutes. Whole hillsides made of loose, dusty soil called loess turned into landslides and slid down over villages.

Somewhere around 200,000 people died, and some estimates are even higher. It struck in the middle of winter, so many of the survivors had lost their homes and then faced freezing temperatures with nowhere to go.

Number 7. The Indian Ocean tsunami, 2004.

On the day after Christmas in 2004, a huge earthquake struck under the sea off the coast of Sumatra. It moved the seafloor so violently that it sent tsunamis racing across the entire Indian Ocean.

Within hours, the waves hit 14 countries, from Indonesia to Thailand, Sri Lanka, India and even the coast of Africa. Around 230,000 people were killed. Many were on beaches that morning with no idea what was coming, because there was no warning system in that ocean.

But there's one part of this story that gives some hope. On a beach in Thailand, a ten-year-old British girl named Tilly Smith saw the sea suddenly pull back and start to bubble. She remembered a geography lesson about tsunamis from just two weeks earlier. She warned her parents, the beach was cleared, and around 100 people survived because one kid paid attention in class.

Number 6. The Tangshan earthquake, China, 1976.

At 3:42 in the morning on the 28th of July 1976, an earthquake struck right underneath the industrial city of Tangshan, home to about a million people. Most of them were asleep.

In about 15 seconds, most of the city's buildings collapsed. The official death toll was around 242,000, and many researchers believe the real number was even higher.

Number 5. The Antioch earthquake, 526 AD.

Now let's go back to the ancient world. Antioch was one of the greatest cities of the Roman Empire, in what is now Turkey. In late May of the year 526, the city was packed with visitors who had come for a religious festival.

Then the earthquake hit. Buildings came down all over the city, and the fires that followed burned for days. Ancient writers say around 250,000 people died. When the news reached Constantinople, the Emperor Justin took off his crown and wore sackcloth in mourning.

It's one of the deadliest earthquakes ever recorded, and it happened almost 1,500 years ago.

Number 4. The Bhola cyclone, 1970.

In November 1970, a powerful cyclone hit the coast of what was then East Pakistan, today's Bangladesh. The land there is very low and flat, and when the storm pushed a huge wall of seawater onto the coast, there was nowhere to run.

Somewhere between 300,000 and 500,000 people died. It's the deadliest tropical cyclone ever recorded.

And the story doesn't end with the storm. Many people were furious about how slowly the government responded. That anger helped fuel the war that, just over a year later, led to Bangladesh becoming an independent country. A storm helped create a nation.

Number 3. The Shaanxi earthquake, China, 1556.

This is the deadliest earthquake in recorded history.

In the early morning of the 23rd of January 1556, a massive quake struck central China. At the time, millions of people in the region lived in yaodongs, homes dug out of the soft loess cliffs, like artificial caves. They were warm and cheap to build, but when the ground shook, the cliffs collapsed and buried the people sleeping inside.

Around 830,000 people died. One scholar who survived wrote some advice afterwards: when an earthquake begins, don't rush outside. Crouch down and wait for the shaking to stop. That's still good advice today, nearly 500 years later.

Number 2. The Yellow River flood, China, 1887.

China's Yellow River is sometimes called China's Sorrow. It carries so much silt that its bed slowly rises, and for centuries people built higher and higher walls along its banks to hold it in. In some places, the river ended up flowing above the land around it.

In September 1887, after heavy rain, those walls broke in Henan province. The water poured out over the flat farmland and spread across a huge area, covering towns and farms.

Somewhere between 900,000 and 2 million people died, from the flood itself and from the famine and disease that followed.

Number 1. The China floods of 1931.

Here's the one that most people have never heard of.

In 1931, after a long drought, central China was hit by extreme snow and then extreme rain. In July alone, several powerful storms swept through. The Yangtze and Huai rivers burst their banks, and so did the Yellow River.

The water covered an area of land bigger than England. The city of Wuhan was underwater for months. Then came the next wave of killers: hunger, cholera and typhoid.

Estimates of the death toll range from around 400,000 to as many as 4 million people. Even at the lowest estimate it's one of the worst disasters in history, and at the highest, it is the deadliest natural disaster ever recorded.

So what do these ten disasters have in common?

Look closely at the list and you'll notice a pattern. Most of the worst disasters didn't kill people directly. Earthquakes don't kill people; falling buildings do. Volcanoes kill people through hunger and tsunamis. Floods kill people through disease, weeks later.

In other words, the deadliest part of a disaster is often what happens after it. And that's the part humans can do something about.

Think about Tilly Smith on that beach. She couldn't stop a tsunami. But she knew one thing, and it saved a hundred lives. Think about the old scholar in China who told people to crouch down instead of running. Knowledge is the only defence that has always worked.

Nature hasn't become any gentler since 1556. But warning systems, stronger buildings and faster rescues mean that disasters which once killed hundreds of thousands now often kill far fewer.

We can't stop earthquakes, floods or volcanoes. But we can outsmart them.
