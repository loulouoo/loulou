A spacecraft worth hundreds of millions of dollars, lost because two teams used different units of measurement. An army that attacked itself in the dark. A man who read the wrong note at a press conference and accidentally knocked down the Berlin Wall. A missing key that might have sunk the Titanic. And an empire that had lasted more than a thousand years, which may have fallen because somebody forgot to lock a door.

These are some of the biggest mistakes in history. Tiny slips, simple errors, and moments of confusion that changed everything.

Today we're counting down the five craziest. And number one shows how a whole empire can come down to one small gate.

Let's start.

Number five. The spacecraft lost to maths.

In December 1998, NASA launched a spacecraft called the Mars Climate Orbiter. Its job was to fly to Mars, go into orbit around the planet and study its weather. The whole mission cost more than 300 million dollars.

For nine months, it flew through space, and everything seemed fine.

Then, in September 1999, it reached Mars and fired its engine to slow down and slip into orbit. And then it disappeared. The signal went silent. It was never heard from again.

So what went wrong?

The spacecraft was built and controlled by two different teams. One team, at a company called Lockheed Martin, wrote software that worked out the push from the spacecraft's thrusters in the old imperial system, using pounds. The other team, at NASA, expected those numbers to be in the metric system, using newtons.

Nobody noticed that the two teams were speaking different maths languages.

So every small correction to the spacecraft's path was slightly wrong. Over nine months and millions of kilometres, all those little errors added up. Instead of passing safely above Mars, the orbiter flew far too low, into the planet's atmosphere, and was destroyed.

A machine that could cross the solar system was lost because nobody checked the units. Every maths teacher who ever told you to write your units down was right.

Number four. The army that attacked itself.

It's September 1788. The Austrian Empire is at war with the Ottoman Empire, and a huge Austrian army is camped near a town called Karánsebes, in what is now Romania.

Now, this story comes to us mostly from later writers, and historians argue about how much of it really happened. But here's how it's usually told.

One night, a group of Austrian cavalry soldiers, called hussars, crossed a river to scout for the enemy. They didn't find any Ottoman soldiers. What they found instead were some local people selling schnapps, a strong alcoholic drink. So the hussars bought it and started a party.

Then some infantry soldiers arrived and wanted to join in. The hussars refused to share. An argument started. Then a fight. Then someone fired a shot.

In the confusion and the dark, somebody started shouting that the Ottomans were coming. They weren't. But panic spread through the army like fire. The Austrian army was made up of soldiers who spoke lots of different languages, and many of them couldn't understand the officers shouting "Halt!" Some thought they were hearing the enemy.

Soldiers started shooting at shadows. Cavalry charged at their own side. Units fled in every direction.

By the time the sun came up, the Austrian army had beaten itself. When the real Ottoman army arrived a couple of days later, they found a camp full of wreckage.

Was it really as bad as the legend says? Probably not. But the story has lasted for more than 200 years for a reason. It's a perfect example of how confusion can be more dangerous than any enemy.

Number three. The note that knocked down the Berlin Wall.

After the Second World War, Germany was split in two. In 1961, East Germany built a wall right through the middle of the city of Berlin, to stop its own people escaping to the West. For 28 years, the Berlin Wall split families apart. People who tried to cross it risked their lives.

By 1989, though, East Germany was in trouble. Huge crowds were protesting in the streets, and thousands of people were escaping through other countries. The government decided to make it a bit easier to travel, hoping that would calm things down. The plan was to bring in new rules, slowly and carefully, starting the next day.

On the evening of the 9th of November 1989, a government spokesman named Günter Schabowski was holding a press conference on live television. Just before he went on, someone handed him a note about the new travel rules.

Nobody told him that the rules weren't supposed to start until the next day.

Near the end of the press conference, he read the note out loud. A reporter asked him the question that changed history: when does this start?

Schabowski looked through his papers, looked confused, and said: as far as I know, immediately. Without delay.

It was a mistake. But people all over East Germany had just heard it on the news.

Within hours, thousands of East Berliners rushed to the border crossings. The guards had no orders. They had no idea what was going on. The crowd kept growing and shouting, "Open the gate!"

At one crossing, the officer in charge finally decided he couldn't hold them back any longer. Late that night, he opened the gate.

People poured through. Strangers hugged and cried. People climbed on top of the wall and started chipping at it with hammers. Within a year, Germany was one country again.

The Berlin Wall was going to fall anyway, at some point. But it fell that night because of one confused man and one note he hadn't been told about properly.

Number two. The key that might have sunk the Titanic.

In April 1912, the Titanic set off on its first voyage, from England to New York. It was the biggest ship in the world.

High up on the ship's mast was a little platform called the crow's nest. Two lookouts stood up there, staring out into the dark, watching for danger. Normally, they would have had binoculars to help them see further.

But there was a problem.

Just before the voyage, the ship's officers were changed around. One officer, named David Blair, was taken off the crew at the last minute. And he left the ship with a small key in his pocket. That key, the story goes, opened the locker where the crow's nest binoculars were kept.

So on the Titanic's first and only voyage, the lookouts had no binoculars.

On the night of the 14th of April, the sea was flat and calm, and there was no moon. That made icebergs very hard to see. By the time lookout Frederick Fleet spotted the iceberg with his bare eyes and rang the bell, it was too late to turn in time.

At the inquiry afterwards, Fleet was asked whether binoculars would have helped. He said they would have seen it sooner. Enough to get out of the way.

Would they have saved the ship? We'll never know for sure. There were lots of other mistakes that night, including ignored ice warnings and not enough lifeboats. But that little key was kept by David Blair's family for nearly a hundred years. In 2007, it was sold at auction for around 90,000 pounds.

The key to the Titanic, sitting in a man's pocket the whole time.

Number one. The gate that ended an empire.

To understand this one, you need to know how old this empire was.

The Roman Empire was split into two halves. The western half fell in the year 476. But the eastern half carried on. We call it the Byzantine Empire, and its capital was the great city of Constantinople, which is now Istanbul in Turkey.

By 1453, this empire had lasted for more than a thousand years. And Constantinople was protected by some of the strongest walls ever built. They had kept out attackers for centuries.

In the spring of 1453, a young Ottoman sultan named Mehmed the Second surrounded the city with a huge army, tens of thousands of soldiers, and massive cannons. Inside, there were only a few thousand defenders.

For seven weeks, the Ottomans attacked again and again. And for seven weeks, the walls held. The defenders fought day and night, repairing the walls as fast as the cannons smashed them.

Then, on the 29th of May 1453, came the final attack.

According to a historian who lived at the time, somewhere along the walls was a small gate, called the Kerkaporta. It was a little side door, used by the defenders to slip out and attack the enemy. And during the chaos of that final morning, someone left it open.

A group of Ottoman soldiers found the open gate, rushed through, climbed up onto the walls and raised their flag. Defenders nearby saw the flag, thought the city had already fallen, and panic spread.

Within hours, Constantinople fell. The last Byzantine emperor died fighting. The empire that had begun with the Romans was over.

Now, historians argue about whether the gate really made the difference. The city was outnumbered, exhausted, and probably going to fall anyway. But the story has been told for nearly 600 years, because it's so hard to forget. A thousand-year-old empire, and its end may have come down to one small door.

So there you have it. A spacecraft lost to the wrong units. An army that attacked itself. A wall that fell because of a misread note. A missing key on the Titanic. And a gate that someone forgot to close.

What these stories show is that history isn't only shaped by great leaders and big battles. Sometimes it's shaped by one person, in one moment, making one tiny mistake.

So next time you forget your keys, or write down the wrong number, don't feel too bad. At least you didn't lose a spacecraft.
