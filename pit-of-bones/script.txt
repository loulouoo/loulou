Picture yourself as a detective. You get called to a crime scene. There are no fingerprints, no witnesses and no suspects. There's no body either, just a skull, broken into 52 pieces.
And the crime didn't happen last night. It happened 430,000 years ago.
That sounds impossible to solve. But scientists took that skull, put it back together piece by piece, and ran it through the same kind of forensic tests that detectives use today. What they found is the oldest known case of one human killing another on purpose.
By the end of this, you'll know how this person died, what the killer was probably like, and what happened to the body afterwards. That last part might be the strangest clue of all.
Before we open the case file, look at the crime scene, because the place where this skull was found is stranger than the murder itself.
In northern Spain, near the city of Burgos, there's a range of hills called Atapuerca. Deep inside one of its caves, past narrow tunnels and crawlspaces, the floor suddenly drops away. There's a vertical shaft about 13 meters deep, as tall as a four-story building, falling straight down into the dark.
At the bottom is a chamber the scientists call Sima de los Huesos. In Spanish, it means "the Pit of Bones."
When researchers started digging there, they found out why. The pit is packed with human remains. It holds thousands of bone fragments from at least 28 different people. There are men, women and teenagers, all crammed into one small chamber, deep underground, where nobody should ever have been.
These weren't modern humans. They were early relatives of the Neanderthals. DNA pulled from their bones later confirmed it, making it some of the oldest human DNA ever recovered.
And there was only one tool in the whole pit: a single, beautifully shaped hand axe made of reddish-pink stone. It's the kind of stone that doesn't come from inside the cave. It was lying among the bones. The scientists nicknamed it "Excalibur."
So you have a hidden pit, 28 bodies and one stone blade.
Now we open the case file.
The victim is known only as Cranium 17. The skull came out of the pit in 52 fragments, recovered over years of digging. Researchers fitted the pieces back together like a jigsaw puzzle, and when they finished, they saw something on the forehead.
There were two holes, right above the left eye.
Now think like a detective. A skull at the bottom of a 13-meter shaft is going to be broken. Bodies fall, rocks fall, and the weight of dirt crushes bone over thousands of years. So the first question is simple: is this damage from the fall, or from something else?
Here's where the forensics come in.
Bone that breaks long after death, once it's dry and brittle, snaps with sharp, straight edges, like old pottery. Bone that breaks while a person is still alive, or right at the moment of death, behaves differently. It's still fresh and flexible, so it bends and fractures in a very particular way.
These two holes were fresh-bone fractures. They happened at the moment this person died, or very close to it. And there was no sign of healing at all. Whatever made these holes, this person never recovered from it.
So it wasn't a crack from the weight of the dirt. Could it have been the fall down the shaft?
That's where the second clue comes in, and it's the one that turns this into a murder case.
The scientists scanned both holes in 3D and compared them. They were almost the same size and almost the same shape. When you line them up, they match, which means they were very likely made by the same object.
But they weren't made from the same direction. Each hole hit the skull at a different angle.
A fall doesn't do that. When a body hits the ground, the skull gets one impact from one direction. It doesn't hit the same rock twice at two different angles, in almost the same spot on the forehead.
What does do that is a person holding a weapon and swinging it twice.
The first blow landed. Then the attacker lifted the weapon again and brought it down a second time. Either blow alone might have been deadly. The fact that there were two tells you this wasn't an accident, and it probably wasn't one lucky strike in a scuffle. Someone meant to finish it.
Now we build a profile of the killer.
Both blows landed on the front of the skull, just above the left eye. That means the attacker was probably standing face to face with the victim. This wasn't someone creeping up from behind. They were close enough to look each other in the eyes.
And think about where a right-handed person naturally strikes when they're facing someone. It's across their body, toward the other person's left side. Most researchers think the attacker was probably right-handed. We can't be certain, but it's exactly the kind of detail a detective would write down.
So here's what we have: two blows with the same weapon, face to face, deliberate and lethal. The weapon was probably something hard and blunt, maybe a wooden club or a stone. Nobody knows who did it or why. It could have been a fight over food, a fight over territory, or something personal. Those parts of the story are gone.
But the case doesn't end with the killing. The strangest clue is what happened to the body.
Think about where this skull was found. It was at the bottom of a pit, deep inside a cave, far from daylight. Nobody lived down there, and animals didn't drag 28 bodies into a vertical shaft. Water didn't wash them in from outside, either.
So how did they get there?
Many researchers think the answer is that someone put them there. They think these early humans carried their dead through the dark tunnels of the cave and dropped them into the pit, one by one, over time. That would make Sima de los Huesos one of the oldest places on Earth where humans deliberately did something with their dead.
Now put that together with the murder. Someone killed this person with two blows to the face. And then, very possibly, someone carried the body deep into the cave and gave it to the pit. It might have been the same group, maybe even the same hands. At the very least, the violence and the caring happened side by side.
And what about Excalibur, the single, beautiful stone axe lying among the bones? Some researchers think it was placed there on purpose, which would make it one of the oldest offerings to the dead ever found.
Now, some scientists will tell you to be careful here. They point out that we can't know for sure how the bodies got into the pit. Maybe some of them fell in. Maybe the bones were moved around by mud and water over hundreds of thousands of years. And one stone axe among the bones could just be a coincidence.
They might be right. The evidence for a funeral is much weaker than the evidence for a murder.
But even if you take the careful view, the core of this case still stands. The two holes are real. The same weapon was swung twice, from two different directions, face to face. 430,000 years ago, one human looked at another and decided to end their life, and then made sure of it.
Here's why that matters.
In our last video, we looked at a Neanderthal named Shanidar 1. He was half-blind and one-armed, and he survived for years because somebody decided he was worth carrying. That's the story we like to tell about our ancestors: that caring for each other is the oldest thing about being human.
And it's true. But it isn't the whole truth.
The bones in the Pit of Bones tell the other half. Right from the very beginning, the same creatures who could carry the weak through the dark could also stand face to face with each other and swing a club twice. Kindness and cruelty didn't arrive at different times in our history. They were both there from the start, in the same species and in the same caves.
Now think about you.
You've probably told yourself that violence is something other people do. Criminals do it, soldiers do it, people in the news do it. You're not like that.
But you're descended from the people in that cave. You're descended from the ones who swung the club and the ones who carried the bodies. Every single human alive today carries both of those instincts, because both of them helped our ancestors survive. The ability to protect your own and the ability to hurt a threat came from the same place.
The difference between them was never in the bones. It was in the choice.
430,000 years ago, in a dark cave in Spain, somebody made the wrong one. And the evidence sat at the bottom of a pit, waiting, until a team of scientists pieced a broken skull back together and finally read the case file.
The oldest murder in human history wasn't solved by finding the killer. It was solved by listening to the victim.
