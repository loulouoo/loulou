31,000 years ago, deep in the rainforest of Borneo, a child lost the bottom part of their left leg.

Not in an accident. Not from an animal attack. Someone cut it off on purpose, cleanly, with stone tools.

And the child survived.

They didn't just survive the surgery. They lived for another six to nine years, in steep jungle mountains, with one leg. And when they finally died, the people around them buried them with care.

This is the oldest known surgery in human history. It's more than 20,000 years older than the one that held the record before it. And it changes what we thought Ice Age people were capable of.

Let's go to Borneo.

Borneo is the third biggest island in the world. It sits in Southeast Asia, shared today by Indonesia, Malaysia and Brunei. Much of it is covered in thick tropical rainforest, full of rivers, giant trees and steep limestone mountains.

In the east of the island, in a region called East Kalimantan, those limestone mountains are full of caves. And these caves are famous among archaeologists. On their walls are some of the oldest paintings ever made by humans. One painting of a wild cow is at least 40,000 years old. The people who lived here were among the first artists on Earth.

In 2020, a team of Australian and Indonesian archaeologists was digging in one of these caves. It's called Liang Tebo. To get to it, you have to travel by boat, then climb up through the forest. Even today, it's not an easy place to reach.

Under the floor of the cave, they found a grave.

Inside was the skeleton of a young person. Scientists call them TB1. They were probably around 19 or 20 years old when they died. The team dated the burial to about 31,000 years ago. That's deep in the last Ice Age, long before farming, long before writing, long before the first cities.

At first it looked like a normal burial. The body had been laid on its back, with its legs pulled up. Rocks had been placed on top of the grave, and pieces of red ochre were nearby. Someone had clearly taken care over this person.

But when the team looked closer, something was missing.

The left foot was gone. And so was the bottom part of the left leg.

Now, bones go missing in old graves all the time. Animals dig them up, water moves them, soil crushes them. So the first question was simple. Did this person lose their leg after they died, or while they were alive?

The answer was in the bone itself.

When the scientists looked at the end of the left leg, they saw that the two lower leg bones, the tibia and the fibula, ended in a clean, smooth stump. The bone had grown over the cut and sealed it. That only happens in a living body. Bone heals slowly, over years. So this person didn't lose their leg after death. They lost it while they were still alive, and then kept living.

The second question was harder. How did they lose it?

If an animal had bitten the leg off, or if it had been crushed by a falling rock, the break would be jagged and messy. You'd expect splinters and crushed bone. But this cut was clean and straight, at a slight angle, right through both bones. That's the kind of cut you get when someone does it on purpose, with a sharp tool.

So this was an amputation. A planned operation.

The third question was the most amazing one. How old was this person when it happened?

By studying how much the bone had healed and grown back, the scientists worked out that the amputation took place around six to nine years before death. The person died at about 19 or 20. That means they were a child, maybe around 10 or 12 years old, when the leg was removed.

So picture this. 31,000 years ago, in a jungle cave, a child is lying on the ground. Someone is holding them down. Someone else picks up a sharp stone blade and starts cutting through skin, muscle, blood vessels and bone.

And the child lives.

Now, why is that so hard to believe?

Because amputation is one of the most dangerous operations there is. Even a few hundred years ago, in Europe, many people died after having a leg taken off. There are three big killers.

The first is blood loss. There are big blood vessels in the leg. If you cut through them and can't stop the bleeding, a person can bleed to death in minutes.

The second is shock. The body can simply shut down from pain and trauma.

The third is infection. A big open wound in a hot, wet rainforest is the perfect place for bacteria to grow. Without antibiotics, infection can spread into the blood and kill someone within days.

The child survived all three.

And there's no sign of infection on the bone at all. If the wound had become badly infected, it would usually leave marks on the bone. There are none. The stump healed cleanly.

So how did they do it?

We have to be honest here. Nobody knows for sure. These people left no instructions. But the skeleton gives us some strong clues.

First, they must have understood anatomy. They had to know where to cut, how to get through the bone, and how to deal with the blood vessels and nerves. You don't learn that by accident. It suggests knowledge passed down over generations.

Second, they must have known how to stop the bleeding. Maybe with pressure, maybe by tying something around the leg, maybe something else we can't see.

Third, they must have kept the wound clean. And here, the rainforest might have helped. Borneo is packed with plants, and some of them have natural chemicals that fight bacteria and dull pain. Local people in Borneo still use plants as medicine today. The researchers think the Ice Age people of Borneo may have used some of these plants too. We can't prove which ones, but something kept that wound clean.

Put all of that together and it means one thing. This wasn't a lucky guess. These people had real medical knowledge.

And the operation was only the beginning.

Think about the years after. This was a child with one leg, living in one of the hardest places on Earth to move around. Steep limestone hills. Thick forest. Slippery rocks. Rivers. These people were hunters and gatherers. They moved around to find food.

A child who couldn't walk properly couldn't keep up on their own. Somebody had to help. Somebody had to carry food to them, wait for them, maybe carry them. And not just for a week, but for years.

That tells us something important about these people. They looked after each other. When someone was hurt, they didn't leave them behind. They cared for them for the rest of their life, and when that life ended, they buried them with respect.

Now let's talk about why this discovery was such a big deal.

Before this skeleton was found, the oldest known amputation came from France. It was a farmer from around 7,000 years ago, who had part of their arm removed and survived.

For a long time, many experts thought that kind of surgery only became possible after farming began. The idea was that when people settled down, built villages and grew crops, they had more time, more food and more stability, and that's when medicine really started to develop.

The child from Borneo destroyed that idea.

This amputation happened more than 20,000 years before the one in France. Before farming. Before villages. It was done by hunter-gatherers living in a rainforest during the Ice Age.

So it turns out that people were doing complex surgery far earlier than anyone thought. Maybe much earlier. Maybe this child wasn't the first. Maybe it's just the first one we've found.

There's still a lot we don't know.

We don't know why the leg was removed. Maybe the child had a terrible injury, like a crushed foot that was starting to rot. Maybe it was an infection or a bite. Whatever it was, the people around them must have decided that cutting off the leg was the only way to save the child's life. And they were right.

We don't know who did the operation, or how many times they had done it before.

And we don't know the child's name, or whether they were a boy or a girl. The skeleton is too damaged to say for certain.

But we do know this. Somewhere in a cave in Borneo, 31,000 years ago, a group of people faced a child who was going to die. And instead of giving up, they made a terrifying decision, they did something incredibly difficult, and it worked.

When we think about the Ice Age, we usually picture people in animal skins, struggling just to survive. Simple people living simple lives.

This child tells a different story. These people were smart. They studied the body. They learned which plants could heal. They passed that knowledge down. And most of all, they cared.

The oldest surgery ever found isn't just a story about stone tools and bone. It's a story about people refusing to let one of their own die.
