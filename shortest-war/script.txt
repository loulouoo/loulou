Picture yourself as a detective. You get handed a case file about a war. Not a war that dragged on for years, with trenches and treaties and millions of soldiers. This one has a start time and an end time written on the same page.

It began at 9:02 in the morning. It was over before 9:45.

That's shorter than a school lesson. Shorter than most movies. You could have missed the whole thing on a long breakfast. And yet hundreds of people were killed or wounded, a palace was destroyed, a ship went to the bottom of a harbour, and a man who called himself a king lost his throne before his tea went cold.

By the end of this, you'll know how a war can be over in under an hour, who started it, who won, and why the losing side ended up getting a bill for the bullets. That last part might be the strangest clue of all.

Before we open the case file, look at the crime scene.

Off the east coast of Africa, in the warm blue Indian Ocean, there's an island called Zanzibar. In the 1800s it was one of the richest places in the region. Ships came from Arabia, India and Europe to trade spices, especially cloves, along with ivory. It was also a centre of something much darker: the slave trade. Thousands of enslaved people were bought and sold in its markets every year.

Zanzibar was ruled by a sultan, who lived in a palace right on the waterfront in Stone Town, the main city. But by 1890, the sultan wasn't really the one in charge. Britain had made Zanzibar a protectorate. In simple words, that meant the sultan kept his title and his palace, but Britain had the final say. And one of the rules was simple: any new sultan needed Britain's approval.

Now we open the case file.

On the 25th of August 1896, the sultan of Zanzibar, Hamad bin Thuwaini, died suddenly in his palace. He was friendly with the British, and they were happy with him. His death was sudden enough that rumours spread fast. Some people whispered that he'd been poisoned. Nobody ever proved it.

But within hours, someone was already moving.

His name was Khalid bin Barghash. He was the dead sultan's cousin, he was 29 years old, and he wanted the throne. Before the British could even react, Khalid walked into the palace complex and declared himself the new sultan of Zanzibar.

Think like a detective here. Khalid knew the rule. He knew Britain had to approve any new sultan, and he knew Britain wanted someone else for the job. So moving in that fast wasn't an accident. It was a gamble. He was betting that if he grabbed the palace first, the British would grumble, complain, and then accept it.

The British did not accept it.

Their man in Zanzibar was a diplomat named Basil Cave. He sent Khalid a message: step down. Khalid refused. Instead, he started getting ready to fight.

He gathered about 2,800 men around the palace. There were palace guards, armed servants and soldiers loyal to him. He had rifles, a few machine guns and some old cannons, some of them pointed out toward the harbour. He even had a navy, of sorts. It was one ship, the sultan's royal yacht, called the Glasgow. It was built in Scotland, painted white, and armed with a handful of small guns.

Then the British started gathering too.

One by one, British warships sailed into the harbour and lined up right in front of the palace. By the next day there were five of them, including a big armoured cruiser called HMS St George, under the command of Rear Admiral Harry Rawson. These were some of the most powerful warships in the world at the time, and they were parked a few hundred metres from a palace made largely of wood.

On the 26th of August, the British gave Khalid an ultimatum. Lower your flag, leave the palace and stand down by 9 o'clock tomorrow morning. If you don't, we open fire.

Now picture the harbour that night. On one side are the warships, their guns already loaded and aimed. On the other side is the palace, with thousands of men behind barricades and the sultan's red flag still flying over it. The city between them is holding its breath.

Early the next morning, at about 8 o'clock, Khalid sent a message to the British. It said he had no intention of lowering his flag, and that he didn't believe they would actually fire on him.

The British replied that they didn't want to fire. But if the flag wasn't down by nine, they would.

That was the last message of the entire war.

At 9 o'clock, the flag was still flying. At about 9:02, the British ships opened fire.

Now here's where the clues start to explain how a war can be this short.

The first clue is the building. The palace was mostly wood. It wasn't built to stand up to modern naval guns, and it didn't. Within minutes, shells were tearing through the walls, and fires broke out across the palace complex. Parts of the building started to collapse with people still inside.

The second clue is the distance. The ships were so close that they barely had to aim. The cannons Khalid's men had set up on the shore were knocked out almost as soon as they fired back.

The third clue is the navy. At one point, the little royal yacht, the Glasgow, fired at HMS St George. It was a brave move, and also a hopeless one. It was like a rowing boat challenging a battleship. The British ships fired back, and the Glasgow was hit, caught fire and sank right there in the shallow harbour, with its masts still sticking out of the water. Its crew raised a British flag to show they were giving up, and British sailors rowed out and pulled them from the water.

Ships against wood, at point-blank range. There was never going to be a long fight.

At around 9:40 in the morning, the sultan's flag came down. Some say it was shot down, others say someone lowered it. Either way, it was the signal. The British stopped firing. The war was over.

Depending on whose clock you trust, it lasted somewhere between 38 and 45 minutes. It's still known as the shortest war in recorded history.

Now we count the damage.

On Khalid's side, about 500 people were killed or wounded. Many of them were men caught inside or around the palace when it collapsed and burned.

On the British side, one sailor was badly wounded. He recovered.

That's what makes this case so strange. It technically counts as a war, but it was really a collision between two completely different worlds: a palace built for an old kind of power, and warships built for a new one.

But the case doesn't end with the shooting. The strangest clue is what happened next.

First, there's the matter of the suspect. Where was Khalid?

As the palace burned, he escaped. He ran to the German consulate in Stone Town and asked for protection. The British wanted him handed over, but the Germans refused. A few weeks later, they smuggled him out to sea on a German boat and took him to German East Africa, on the mainland. For years, he lived there out of Britain's reach.

He wasn't free forever, though. Twenty years later, during the First World War, British forces captured him. He was sent into exile far away, first on the Seychelles islands and then on Saint Helena, the same remote island where Napoleon had been sent. Eventually he was allowed to return to East Africa, and he died in Mombasa, in Kenya, in 1927.

Second, there's the new sultan. That very same afternoon, the British put their own choice on the throne, a man named Hamoud. And within a year, under British pressure, slavery was officially abolished in Zanzibar. So a 40-minute war helped push along the end of something that had lasted for centuries.

And third, there's the bill.

After the shooting stopped, Britain decided Zanzibar should pay for the war. That included the cost of the shells the British ships had fired at the palace. Khalid's supporters were made to pay for the ammunition that had been used against them.

Think about that. You lose a war in 40 minutes, and then you get the invoice for it.

Now, some historians will tell you to be careful here. They point out that calling this a "war" is a bit of a stretch. It was more like a short, brutal punishment by an empire against a much weaker ruler who had broken its rules. There was no long campaign and no real chance for the other side.

They might be right. But even if you take the careful view, the core of this case still stands. A man made a gamble, a flag stayed up a few minutes too long, and around 500 people paid for it in less time than it takes to watch a TV episode.

Here's why that matters.

In our last video, we looked at Jebel Sahaba, the oldest war ever found. That one wasn't one battle. It was raid after raid along the Nile, generation after generation, for so long that fighting became a way of life.

The Anglo-Zanzibar War is the opposite. It shows how much changed in 13,000 years. Back then, war was slow, close and personal, spears and arrows by a river. By 1896, one side could sit in the harbour, never set foot on the beach, and flatten a palace before the morning was out.

Same species, same choices, but very different tools. And the more powerful the tools became, the faster a bad decision could turn into a disaster.

Now think about you.

You've probably made a decision in a hurry. You grabbed something first, said something first, or acted first, hoping everyone else would just go along with it. Most of the time, nothing happens.

Khalid made the same kind of bet. He moved fast, grabbed the palace and dared the other side to do something about it. He didn't believe they would fire. And he was wrong, by about two minutes past nine.

The lesson isn't that the strongest side always wins. It's that the most dangerous moment is the one where somebody refuses to back down because they're sure the other side won't follow through.

The shortest war in history wasn't decided by an army. It was decided by one flag, one deadline and one man who thought nobody would call his bluff.
