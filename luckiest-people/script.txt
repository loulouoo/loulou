Number five. The woman hit by a meteorite.

It's the 30th of November 1954, in a small town called Sylacauga, in Alabama, in the United States. A woman named Ann Hodges is feeling unwell, so she lies down on her sofa for an afternoon nap, under a blanket.

Then, BANG.

A rock about the size of a grapefruit, weighing around four kilograms, smashes through the roof of her house. It bursts through the ceiling, bounces off a big wooden radio, and slams into Ann's hip.

She wakes up in pain, the room full of dust, with a huge black-and-blue bruise down her side.

The rock had come from space. It was a meteorite. Millions of meteorites fall to Earth, but almost all of them land in the sea, in deserts, or in empty fields. Ann Hodges is the first person in history confirmed to have been hit by one.

Think about how unlikely that is. Out of the whole surface of the Earth, that rock picked her house, her room, her sofa.

And the story gets stranger. Ann became famous overnight. Reporters crowded outside. The government took the meteorite away to study it. Then Ann's landlady said the rock belonged to her, because it had landed on her property. They ended up arguing about it for a year.

Meanwhile, a local farmer found another piece of the same meteorite in a field nearby. He sold it and, the story goes, bought himself a car and a house.

So one person got hit and got a bruise, and the other just picked his piece up off the ground and got rich. That's luck for you.

Number four. The man who was struck by lightning seven times.

Roy Sullivan was a park ranger in the Shenandoah National Park, in Virginia. It's a beautiful place, full of mountains, forests and lookout towers. It's also a place with a lot of thunderstorms.

The chance of an ordinary person being struck by lightning in their lifetime is something like one in fifteen thousand. Roy Sullivan was struck seven times.

The first time was in 1942, in a lookout tower. The lightning burned a strip down his leg and took off his big toenail.

The second time, in 1969, he was driving his truck on a mountain road. Lightning hit, knocked him out, and burned off his eyebrows.

The third time, in his garden, it burned his shoulder.

The fourth time, it set his hair on fire. After that, he started carrying a can of water with him, just in case.

The fifth time, he saw a storm cloud coming and tried to drive away from it. The cloud seemed to follow him. When he finally got out of the truck, it hit him and set his hair on fire again.

Then a sixth time. And a seventh, in 1977, while he was fishing.

He survived every single one. He's in the Guinness World Records as the person struck by lightning more times than anyone else. And his ranger hats, burned by lightning, ended up on display in museums.

People started to avoid standing next to him during storms. Can you blame them?

Number three. The woman who survived three shipwrecks.

Violet Jessop was a stewardess and nurse who worked on ocean liners in the early 1900s. And she had a very unusual career.

In 1911 she was working on a giant new ship called the Olympic. On one of its first trips, it crashed into a British warship. The Olympic was badly damaged, but it made it back to port. Nobody was killed.

The next year, Violet got a job on the Olympic's brand-new sister ship. It was the biggest ship in the world, and people said it was unsinkable.

It was called the Titanic.

On the night of the 14th of April 1912, the Titanic hit an iceberg and began to sink. Violet was ordered up on deck to help calm the passengers. She was put into a lifeboat, and as it was lowered, someone handed her a baby to look after. She spent the night in the freezing ocean, holding that baby, until a rescue ship arrived the next morning. More than 1,500 people died that night. Violet survived.

Then came the First World War. The third sister ship, the Britannic, was turned into a hospital ship, and Violet worked on board as a nurse. In 1916, the Britannic hit a mine and began to sink.

Violet jumped into a lifeboat, but the lifeboat was being sucked towards the ship's giant spinning propellers. She jumped into the sea, was pulled under the water, and hit her head hard on the bottom of the ship. She survived that too.

And here's the funny part. When she later talked about the Britannic, she said that as it sank, she ran back to her cabin to grab one thing. Her toothbrush. Because she remembered how much she'd missed it after the Titanic.

Three ships. Three disasters. Violet Jessop lived to the age of 83.

Number two. The flight attendant who fell ten kilometres.

On the 26th of January 1972, a passenger plane was flying high over Czechoslovakia, on its way from Copenhagen. On board was a 22-year-old flight attendant from Yugoslavia named Vesna Vulović.

Vesna wasn't even supposed to be on that flight. The airline had mixed her up with another flight attendant who had the same first name.

At about ten kilometres up, higher than Mount Everest, a bomb hidden on the plane exploded. The plane broke apart in the sky.

Everyone else on board was killed. But Vesna was trapped inside a section of the plane, held in by a food trolley. That piece of the plane fell all the way to the ground and crashed onto a snowy, wooded mountainside, where the trees and the snow helped to slow it down.

A man who lived nearby found her, alive, and helped her until rescuers came. She had a fractured skull, broken legs and a broken back. She was in a coma for days.

But she lived. She even learned to walk again. And she's in the Guinness World Records for surviving the highest fall without a parachute, more than ten thousand metres.

Some investigators have since argued the plane might have been lower when it broke up. But even if they're right, she still fell from a height that should have been impossible to survive.

And when people asked her how she felt about it, she said she didn't really feel lucky. She said everyone else on that plane had died, and she was the one left to remember it.

Number one. The girl who fell into the Amazon.

It's Christmas Eve, 1971, in Peru, in South America. A seventeen-year-old girl named Juliane Koepcke is on a plane with her mother. They're flying to see her father, a scientist who works at a research station deep in the Amazon rainforest.

About half an hour into the flight, the plane flies into a huge thunderstorm. Lightning hits it. The plane breaks apart in the air.

Juliane suddenly finds herself outside the plane, still strapped into her row of seats, spinning down towards the jungle, from about three kilometres up. She later remembered seeing the treetops below her, looking like broccoli. Then she blacked out.

She woke up the next morning on the forest floor, still in her seat. She had a broken collarbone, a deep cut on her arm, and one eye swollen shut. She had lost her glasses and one of her sandals. And she was completely alone.

Her mother was gone. Everyone else from the plane was gone. She was the only survivor out of 92 people.

Now, most people would have no idea what to do next. But Juliane had grown up at her parents' research station in the jungle. And her father had taught her something very important.

If you're ever lost in the jungle, find water. Follow a stream. Streams lead to rivers, and rivers lead to people.

So that's what she did. She found a small stream, and she followed it. Day after day, walking, wading, and sometimes floating down the water. She was hungry, she was hurt, and the jungle was full of insects, snakes and stingrays. She had only a small bag of sweets she'd found among the wreckage.

On the tenth day, she found a little boat and a hut on the riverbank. She waited there. The next day, a group of local forest workers came back to the hut and found her. They thought, at first, that she might be a spirit. They cleaned her wounds, fed her, and took her to safety.

She had survived a fall from the sky, and then eleven days alone in the Amazon.

And here's the best part of her story. Juliane didn't stay away from the jungle that nearly killed her. She grew up to become a biologist, just like her parents, and she went on to look after the very same rainforest research station. She spent her life protecting the place where she survived.
