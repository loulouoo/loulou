# The First Dog Was a Terrible Idea: script

Rewritten from the "super predator" script: same pacing and "you" voice, same structure (cold open, strange finds in the ground, the "how", the big experiment, the turn on the viewer). Roughly the same length (~2,100 words, about 13 to 14 min at the same pace).

---

Picture yourself 30,000 years ago, sitting by a fire at the edge of a frozen world. It's the middle of winter. Everything you own is in this camp: your food, your tools, your children asleep behind you. And out there, just past where the firelight ends, there are eyes. Wolves. A whole pack of them. They hunt the same animals you hunt. They have jaws that can crack a leg bone like a twig. They can smell your meat from miles away, and they can see in the dark when you can't.

Now imagine someone in your group stands up, walks toward those eyes, and throws them a piece of meat.

That is the worst idea anyone has ever had. You don't feed the thing that wants to eat what you eat. You don't invite a predator to dinner. And yet somebody did. And today, there are around 900 million descendants of those wolves on this planet, and one of them might be asleep on your bed right now. By the end of this, you'll understand why that terrible idea might be the reason you exist at all.

But before I show you how a wolf ended up on your couch, look at something stranger. Because the first clue isn't in how dogs behave. It's in the things we buried with them.

In 1914, two quarry workers in a place called Oberkassel, near Bonn in Germany, broke into an old grave. Inside were a man, a woman, and the bones of a dog. The grave was around 14,000 years old. For a century, nobody looked very closely at that dog. Then a team of scientists studied its teeth and found something no one expected. This dog had been very sick. It had canine distemper, a disease that even today kills puppies within weeks. It would have had fevers, seizures, diarrhea. It couldn't have hunted. It couldn't have guarded anything. It was useless.

And it lived. It survived the sickness for weeks, maybe months, which is almost impossible unless someone was keeping it warm, keeping it clean, and feeding it by hand. Somebody sat up with a sick puppy through the Ice Age, when food was scarce and every mouthful mattered. And when it finally died, they buried it next to their own people.

But that dog at least had someone who loved it. The next one is harder to explain.

In the Czech Republic, at a site called Předmostí, archaeologists dug up the skulls of large canines from around 30,000 years ago. Some scientists think these were early dogs. Others think they were just wolves. Nobody has settled it. But one of those skulls had something that doesn't belong there. A piece of bone, most likely from a mammoth, had been pushed deliberately between its teeth after it died. Someone placed it there, like giving it something to carry into whatever came next. No wolf does that to another wolf. Only a person would.

And it goes further back than dogs we can even prove. In Israel, at a site called Ain Mallaha, there's a grave around 12,000 years old. Inside is an elderly person lying on their side, and their hand is resting on a puppy. Not thrown in beside them. Their hand is placed on it, the way you'd rest your hand on a dog asleep next to you. Whoever buried them wanted them to stay touching.

No other animal buries another species. No other animal nurses a sick predator back to health or puts its hand on a wolf's child for eternity. Somewhere along the way, two of the most dangerous hunters on Earth stopped being enemies. The question is how. Because on paper, this should never have worked.

Here's the problem. Wolves and humans were competitors. We chased the same reindeer, the same horses, the same bison across the same frozen plains. Every animal a wolf pack killed was one you didn't get to eat. And wolves are good at it. They're faster than you, they bite harder than you, and a pack of them working together can bring down a moose. When two predators hunt the same food, the normal answer in nature is simple. One chases the other off, or kills it.

So why would a starving Ice Age human ever share?

The answer might be hidden in what you can't eat. Humans can't live on lean meat alone. If most of what you eat is pure protein without enough fat, your body starts to break down. People have called it rabbit starvation: you can eat and eat and still get weaker. So in the deepest part of winter, Ice Age hunters had a strange problem. They'd kill big animals, eat the fat, the marrow, and the organs, and be left with piles of lean meat they couldn't safely eat much more of.

But a wolf can. A wolf can live on lean meat for months and be completely fine. Some researchers think that's the trick. In the hardest months of the year, feeding wolves didn't cost people anything, because the wolves were eating the part people couldn't use. It wasn't generosity. It was leftovers.

Some scientists think it happened a different way: that wolves weren't invited at all, they just started showing up to eat our trash. They argue about it, and they might never settle it. But either way, it shows you the start of the deal. The first dogs weren't pets. They were the wolves that got close enough to the fire to eat what we threw away.

But getting close to humans is dangerous. So only one kind of wolf could make it work.

Picture a pack circling a camp. Most of the wolves hang back in the dark. They're scared of people and they have every reason to be. But one or two of them are different. They're a little less afraid. They come a little closer. They take the scraps. And because they get fed, they survive the winter when the others don't, and they have pups. And some of those pups are a little less afraid too.

We know how fast this can happen, because in 1959, a scientist in the Soviet Union named Dmitry Belyaev tried it with foxes. He started breeding silver foxes and kept only one rule. He picked the calmest, friendliest ones in each generation, and nothing else. Not the prettiest, not the biggest. Just the least afraid of people.

Within about ten generations, some of the foxes were wagging their tails when a person walked by. They whined for attention. They licked people's hands. And then their bodies started to change, even though no one was selecting for that. Some of them got floppy ears. Their tails started to curl. Patches of white appeared in their fur. Their faces got shorter and cuter. Just by choosing for friendliness, Belyaev had started turning wild foxes into something that looked and acted like a dog, in a single human lifetime.

Now give the wolves thousands of years instead of decades.

Look at a dog's face. When your dog wants something, it does a thing with its eyes. The inner corner of its eyebrow lifts up, which makes the eyes look bigger and sadder, like a baby's. You know exactly the look I mean. In 2019, scientists dissected the faces of dogs and wolves and found that dogs have a small muscle right above the eye that does exactly that. Wolves mostly don't have it. That muscle may have appeared because every time a dog made that face, a human gave it food. We didn't mean to. We just couldn't say no.

And it goes deeper than the face. When you and a dog look into each other's eyes, both of your brains release oxytocin, the same chemical that bonds a mother to her baby. Scientists in Japan measured it. Owners and dogs who stared at each other had their oxytocin go up, both of them. When they tried the same thing with wolves raised by humans, the effect didn't show up. The dog has somehow hacked into the system your brain uses to love its own children.

Point at something. Just point across the room. A chimpanzee, your closest living relative, is mostly bad at understanding what that means. A wolf raised by humans usually looks at your hand. But a puppy, a few weeks old, that has barely met a person, will turn its head and look where you're pointing. Dogs are born reading us.

So the wolf changed its body, its face, and its brain to fit into our lives. But here's the part people forget. We changed for them too. Because once they were in the camp, everything about how we lived shifted.

Now picture a hunt with a dog by your side. The thing humans were always bad at was finding things. You have maybe 6 million smell receptors in your nose. A dog has somewhere around 300 million. You can walk right past a hidden deer. A dog can't. You're good at throwing a spear, planning an ambush, carrying on for hours. The dog is good at tracking, cornering, and holding an animal in place until you get there.

In the desert of Saudi Arabia, at a place called Shuwaymis, there are rock carvings thousands of years old that show exactly this: a hunter with a bow, and a pack of dogs surrounding an animal. And some of those dogs have lines running from their necks to the hunter's waist. They're leashes. It may be the oldest picture of a leash ever found.

At night, the dogs heard things we couldn't. A human camp with dogs was a camp that couldn't be crept up on. Something moving in the dark, a bear, a lion, another tribe, and the dogs would know long before you did.

Some researchers go even further. One scientist, Pat Shipman, has argued that humans teaming up with wolf-dogs might be part of the reason we outlasted the Neanderthals in Europe. A group of humans with dogs could find prey faster, hunt it more safely, and guard their kills. It's a controversial idea, and plenty of experts don't buy it. But even if it's wrong, it shows you what the deal really was. It wasn't one species taming another. It was two hunters merging into a single team, each covering for what the other was missing.

And you can see how well that deal worked if you look at what happened to everyone who didn't take it.

Today there are somewhere around 200,000 to 250,000 wild wolves left on the entire planet. They were hunted, poisoned, and pushed out of almost everywhere humans live. The wolves that stayed afraid of us, the ones that stayed in the dark, nearly got wiped out.

And the ones that walked up to the fire? There are somewhere around 900 million of them. They live on every continent where people live. They sleep in our beds. They've even adapted to our food. Dogs carry extra copies of a gene that helps digest starch, something wolves are bad at, because for thousands of years they were eating what farmers ate. They are one of the most successful large mammals on Earth, and they got there by being on our side.

The terrible idea turned out to be the smartest decision either species ever made.

So think about that night again. The fire, the frozen dark, the eyes just past the light. Someone threw a piece of meat. And one wolf, a little braver than the rest, stepped forward to take it.

You come from the people who didn't chase it away. And the dog at your feet comes from the wolf that didn't run.

Every time your dog tilts its head at your voice, every time it looks at you with those big eyes and you give in, every time it follows your finger across the room, you're watching the end of a deal that was made in the dark more than 15,000 years ago.

We spent a whole video last time talking about how every animal on Earth learned to fear us. But there's one that looked at the most dangerous creature on the planet and decided to trust it anyway. And for thousands of years, we've been trying to deserve that.

## Facts to check before recording

All of these are my summaries of published research and are worth verifying against the original papers before narrating:

- **Bonn-Oberkassel dog.** ~14,000 years old, found 1914, sick with canine distemper and cared for (Janssens et al., 2018, *Journal of Archaeological Science*).
- **Předmostí skull with bone in mouth.** ~30,000 years old; it's debated whether it was a dog or a wolf (Germonpré et al., 2012).
- **Ain Mallaha burial.** ~12,000 years old; human hand resting on a puppy (Davis & Valla, 1978, *Nature*).
- **Lean-meat surplus hypothesis.** Lahtinen et al., 2021, *Scientific Reports*.
- **Belyaev fox experiment.** Began 1959; tail wagging within ~10 generations; later floppy ears, curly tails and piebald coats.
- **Puppy-eyes muscle.** Kaminski et al., 2019, *PNAS*.
- **Oxytocin gaze loop.** Nagasawa et al., 2015, *Science*.
- **Pointing.** Hare et al., 2002 (dogs vs. chimps/wolves); later studies show puppies following pointing with little human contact.
- **Smell receptors.** ~300 million (dog) vs ~6 million (human) is the commonly quoted figure.
- **Shuwaymis rock art with leashes.** Guagnin et al., 2018, *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology*; dated roughly 8,000 to 9,000 years.
- **Pat Shipman.** *The Invaders* (2015); a controversial hypothesis.
- **Dog/wolf numbers.** ~900 million dogs and ~200,000 to 250,000 wolves are rough estimates.
- **Starch gene (AMY2B).** Axelsson et al., 2013, *Nature*.
- **Domestication date.** Estimates range from roughly 15,000 to 40,000 years ago, and "more than 15,000 years" is the safe floor. Keep the opening's "30,000 years ago" framed as a scene, not a hard date.
