# Neanderthals vs Us: script

Rewritten from the "super predator" script: same pacing and "you" voice, same structure (cold open with a promise, strange finds in the ground, the "how" in three parts, a twist, then the turn on the viewer). About 2,100 words, roughly 13 to 14 min at the same pace.

---

Picture yourself 45,000 years ago, walking into a frozen valley somewhere in Europe. You're cold. You're hungry. You're a long way from where your people came from. And on the other side of the river, someone is watching you.

He's shorter than you, but he's built like a wall. Thick arms, a barrel chest, hands that could crush yours. His people have lived in this valley for longer than you can imagine. Not hundreds of years. Hundreds of thousands. They've survived ice ages that would kill you in a week. And his brain, the thing you think makes you special, is actually bigger than yours.

He's a Neanderthal. And by every measure that should matter, he's going to win.

Now look around. He's gone. Every single one of them is gone. And you're still here. By the end of this, you'll understand how the weaker, skinnier, newer human beat the strongest people who ever lived, and why a part of them is still inside your body right now.

But before I show you how that happened, you need to forget everything you've been told about Neanderthals. Because the cartoon version, the grunting caveman dragging a club, is wrong. And the proof is buried in the ground.

In the south of France, there's a cave called Bruniquel. In 1990, a teenager squeezed through a gap in the rocks and found something nobody expected, more than 300 metres into total darkness. Someone had snapped off around 400 stalagmites, the stone spikes that grow up from cave floors, and arranged them into rings. Big, deliberate circles, stacked like walls. And there were burn marks on them. Someone had brought fire down there.

When scientists finally dated it, they got a number that made no sense. 176,000 years old. Our species wasn't anywhere near Europe then. The only humans in France were Neanderthals. Which means a Neanderthal walked deep into the dark, carrying fire, and built something. We don't know what it was for. But nobody builds circles in the dark for no reason.

And that's not the only thing they built.

In Croatia, at a site called Krapina, archaeologists found eight eagle talons, about 130,000 years old. They had cut marks on them and polished spots, like they'd rubbed against each other for a long time. Most researchers think they were strung together and worn. A necklace. Made from the claws of an eagle, the deadliest bird in the sky. By people we used to call brutes.

But jewellery is still just decoration. The next thing is harder to explain.

In northern Spain, in a cave called El Sidrón, scientists scraped the hardened plaque off the teeth of a Neanderthal who lived around 49,000 years ago. Plaque traps tiny bits of everything you eat. And in his, they found DNA from poplar, a tree whose bark contains the natural chemical that aspirin is based on. They also found traces of a mould related to the one penicillin comes from.

Here's the part that matters. This man had a painful abscess in his jaw and a parasite in his gut. He was sick. And it looks like he was chewing the bark of a painkiller tree. Some researchers think he was medicating himself. Others say it could be a coincidence. But either way, he wasn't just surviving. He may have been treating himself.

They made glue by heating birch bark at exactly the right temperature. They twisted plant fibres into three-ply string. They buried their dead. They took care of their injured. We covered one of them, a man called Shanidar 1, in a whole video. He was blind in one eye, missing an arm, half deaf, and he lived for years because his people looked after him.

So this was never smart humans versus dumb cavemen. It was two kinds of smart people, meeting in the same frozen world. Only one of them made it out. To understand why, you have to look at what each of them was built for.

Start with the body. A Neanderthal was a cold-weather machine. Short limbs and a thick, compact body to hold in heat. Huge muscles. Bones so thick they look like they belonged to professional athletes. A Neanderthal woman could probably have beaten most men alive today in an arm-wrestle.

But that body came with a price. All that muscle burns fuel, even when you're sitting still. Some estimates say a Neanderthal needed somewhere around 3,500 to 5,000 calories a day just to stay alive in the cold. That's like eating seven or eight big meals every single day, in a world with no shops, no farms, and no fridges. Every day they didn't find food, their own bodies started eating them.

And look at how they got that food. Neanderthal bones are covered in old injuries. Broken ribs, cracked skulls, fractured arms. Back in the 1990s, two scientists noticed that the pattern of injuries matched one modern group almost perfectly: rodeo riders. People who get thrown around by huge, angry animals up close. Later studies have argued about how unusual their injuries really were. But it fits what their weapons tell us. Neanderthals mostly hunted with heavy spears, close up. They walked right up to a bison or a woolly rhino and stabbed it.

Now look at you. Your body is a terrible cold-weather machine. You're long and skinny, built for the heat of Africa, where your species came from. You're weaker. You'd lose that arm-wrestle.

But you need less fuel. And you had something else. Remember the throwing arm from our last video? The shoulder that works like a slingshot? Modern humans took that and went further. Spear-throwers that launch a dart harder than any arm could. And later, the bow. You didn't have to walk up to the woolly rhino. You could kill it from across the clearing and walk home without a broken rib.

So on one side, the strongest humans who ever lived, fighting their food up close and burning through calories they couldn't afford. On the other side, a weaker human who could fight from a distance on half the fuel. But muscles and spears still don't explain it. Because the biggest difference wasn't in their bodies. It was in their heads.

Here's something strange. A Neanderthal brain was bigger than yours, on average. So how does the bigger brain lose?

Some scientists think the answer is in how that brain was being used. Neanderthals had larger eye sockets and bigger bodies to control. One idea is that more of their brain was taken up by vision and moving that powerful body, which left less room for something else. And that something else is the thing that made you dangerous in the first place. Other people.

Look at how they lived. When scientists read Neanderthal DNA, they find something worrying. Very little variety. Their populations were tiny. In a cave in Siberia called Chagyrskaya, researchers found the remains of a group of about a dozen Neanderthals. A father and his teenage daughter were among them. And the DNA suggests groups like this were very small, about 20 people, isolated, with only a few outsiders joining over time. Another Neanderthal from the Altai mountains had parents who were as closely related as half-siblings.

Now look at the things they used. The stone for Neanderthal tools almost always came from close by. Within a day or two's walk.

Now look at you. Early modern humans in Europe were wearing seashells from coasts hundreds of kilometres away. They were moving stone across huge distances. That doesn't mean one person walked all that way. It means they had networks. Friends of friends of friends. Groups that met up, swapped things, swapped stories, swapped partners.

And remember what that means. In the last video we talked about how humans hunt in "we mode", one mind spread across many bodies. Now stretch that mind across a whole continent. A Neanderthal group of 20 knew what 20 people knew. If a new trick was invented, it might die with whoever invented it. But a modern human connected to hundreds of other groups had a backup of everything. When one group figured out a better needle, a better trap, a better way to survive a terrible winter, it spread. When one group starved, another group could help.

So when the climate in Europe started swinging wildly between cold and colder, a Neanderthal band was on its own. Your ancestors were part of something much bigger.

But here's the twist. Even with all of that, we didn't win the first time.

In the south of France, there's a rock shelter called Grotte Mandrin. People lived there on and off for tens of thousands of years, and their trash is stacked in layers, like the pages of a book. In 2022, scientists published what they found in one thin layer. Neanderthal tools below it. Neanderthal tools above it. And in the middle, a tooth from a modern human child, with tiny stone points that look nothing like what Neanderthals made. About 54,000 years old.

It means modern humans reached Europe much earlier than we thought. And then they disappeared. The Neanderthals came back and lived in that same shelter for thousands more years.

So this wasn't a straight line. We showed up, and we failed. We came back, maybe more than once. And somewhere around 40,000 years ago, after the two kinds of human had shared Europe for thousands of years, the Neanderthals were gone.

Some scientists think we pushed them out by competing for the same animals. Others think disease, climate, or just bad luck finished off a population that was already tiny. Most think it was a mix of everything. They argue about it, and they might never settle it.

But the most important thing we've found isn't how they disappeared. It's that they didn't completely disappear at all.

In a cave in Romania called Peștera cu Oase, the Cave of Bones, explorers found a jawbone around 40,000 years old. It belonged to a modern human. But when scientists read its DNA, they found that somewhere between 6 and 9% of it came from Neanderthals. Much more than anyone alive today. It meant this man had a Neanderthal ancestor just four to six generations back. His great-great-great-grandparent was a Neanderthal.

The two kinds of human didn't just fight. They met. They had children. And those children had children.

In 2010, scientists finished reading the first Neanderthal genome. And they found that if your family comes from anywhere outside Africa, around 1 to 2% of your DNA came from Neanderthals. Some of it still affects your immune system. Some of it affects your skin and hair. In 2020, scientists even found that a stretch of Neanderthal DNA many people carry raised the risk of getting seriously ill with COVID. A choice two people made 50,000 years ago was still showing up in hospitals in the 21st century.

So think about that valley again. The river, the cold, the man watching you from the other side. You spent this whole video thinking it was a fight. That one side won and one side lost.

But some of those meetings didn't end in a fight. Some of them ended in a family. Some of the strongest people who ever lived didn't vanish. They walked across that river, and they came home with us.

Every time your body fights off an infection, every time you look in the mirror at the skin and hair you were born with, there's a chance a Neanderthal is part of the reason. They survived 300,000 years of ice. They built circles in the dark. They took care of their injured. And the last of them is still here, inside you, right where you're sitting.

We didn't just beat the Neanderthals. In a way, we became them.

## Facts to check before recording

All from memory. Check them against the sources before narrating:

- **Neanderthal timeline:** in Europe and western Asia from around 400,000 years ago, gone around 40,000 years ago (Higham et al., 2014, *Nature*). Overlap with modern humans of a few thousand years.
- **Brain size:** Neanderthal average slightly larger (~1,400 cc vs ~1,350 cc for recent humans). Figures vary by source.
- **Bruniquel Cave:** stalagmite structures dated ~176,000 years, about 336 m into the cave, ~400 pieces, burn marks (Jaubert et al., 2016, *Nature*). Found by a teenager in 1990.
- **Krapina eagle talons:** ~130,000 years, eight talons with cut marks and polish (Radovčić et al., 2015, *PLOS ONE*).
- **El Sidrón dental calculus:** poplar DNA and *Penicillium* traces in an individual with a dental abscess and a gut parasite, ~49,000 years (Weyrich et al., 2017, *Nature*). The self-medication reading is debated.
- **Birch tar and three-ply cord:** birch tar from Campitelli, Italy (~200,000 years); three-ply cord fragment from Abri du Maras, France (Hardy et al., 2020, *Scientific Reports*).
- **Calories:** estimates of ~3,500 to 5,000 kcal/day vary widely between studies.
- **Rodeo-rider injuries:** Berger & Trinkaus (1995). Later work (Beier et al., 2018, *Nature*) found Neanderthal injury rates similar to those of Upper Palaeolithic modern humans. The script already flags the debate.
- **Vision/brain hypothesis:** Pearce, Stringer & Dunbar (2013), *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*. A hypothesis, not settled.
- **Chagyrskaya:** remains of ~11 to 13 individuals including a father and teenage daughter, with communities of ~20 inferred (Skov et al., 2022, *Nature*).
- **Altai Neanderthal:** parents as related as half-siblings (Prüfer et al., 2014, *Nature*).
- **Local raw materials vs long-distance networks:** Neanderthal materials mostly local; modern human shell ornaments and stone moved hundreds of km. Commonly reported, so check specific examples if you want to name any.
- **Grotte Mandrin:** modern human presence ~54,000 years, sandwiched between Neanderthal layers (Slimak et al., 2022, *Science Advances*). The child's tooth is the key evidence.
- **Peștera cu Oase:** Oase 1 jaw, ~37,000 to 42,000 years, 6 to 9% Neanderthal DNA, ancestor 4 to 6 generations back (Fu et al., 2015, *Nature*).
- **Neanderthal DNA today:** ~1 to 2% in people with ancestry from outside Africa (Green et al., 2010, *Science*, and later studies). African populations also carry some via back-migration, so the script says "if your family comes from anywhere outside Africa" to stay safe.
- **COVID risk region:** Zeberg & Pääbo (2020), *Nature*.
