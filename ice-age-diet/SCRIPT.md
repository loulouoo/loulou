# The Ice Age Diet: script

Rewritten from the "super predator" script: same pacing and "you" voice, same structure (cold open with a promise, strange finds in the ground, the "how" in three parts, a twist, then the turn on the viewer). About 1,760 words, roughly 11 to 12 min at the same pace.

---

Picture yourself 30,000 years ago, sitting by a fire in the middle of an Ice Age winter. Snow up to your knees. Wind that cuts through your furs. You're starving. And everyone has told you what happens next. You grab a spear, you take down a woolly mammoth, and you tear into a giant steak like a caveman in a cartoon.

Except that's not what's on the menu.

What's actually in front of you is stranger than anything in a cartoon. Mushrooms. Crab. Bone grease boiled out of a skeleton. Maybe even something that looks a lot like a pancake. By the end of this, you'll understand why the "caveman diet" people sell today is mostly made up, and why the sandwich you ate for lunch is a recipe older than farming itself.

But before I show you what was on that menu, look at something stranger. Because the first clue to how our ancestors really ate isn't in their spears. It's in their trash.

In the desert of northeast Jordan, there's a site called Shubayqa 1. Around 14,400 years ago, a group of hunter-gatherers lived there, people who had never planted a single seed. They built a stone fireplace. And in 2018, scientists sifting through the ashes found tiny black crumbs. Under a microscope, the crumbs had air bubbles in them. They'd been ground, mixed with water, shaped and baked.

It was bread. Flatbread, made from wild grain and the roots of a marsh plant. And it was baked about 4,000 years before anyone in that region started farming. People didn't invent farming and then make bread. They might have made bread first, and liked it so much that they started growing the plants to make more.

But at least bread makes sense to us. The next one is harder to swallow.

In Israel, there's a cave called Qesem. People lived in it around 400,000 years ago, long before our species existed. And in the cave, archaeologists found deer leg bones with strange cut marks on them. The cuts weren't where you'd slice off meat. They were where you'd peel off dry skin. That only happens if the bone has been left sitting, with the skin still on, for a while.

When scientists tested it, they found that a deer leg left like that keeps the marrow inside fresh and edible for weeks. The skin works like a wrapper. These people weren't just eating their kill. They were storing it. Keeping bones in the cave and cracking them open later, when the hunting was bad. It's the oldest evidence of saving food for later. Some researchers have called it the first canned food.

And it gets stranger. In Germany, at a place called Neumark-Nord, archaeologists dug up the remains of a lakeshore where Neanderthals worked around 125,000 years ago. And they found bones. Not a few bones. Bones from more than 170 large animals, deer, horses and cattle, all smashed into tiny pieces.

It took scientists years to figure out why. The Neanderthals had broken the bones up on purpose and boiled them, for hours, to melt out the fat hidden inside. It was a factory. A fat factory, by a lake, 125,000 years ago. Fat was the most precious food in the Ice Age, and they built a whole operation to squeeze every drop of it out of a skeleton.

But boiling bones still sounds like survival. The last one sounds like dinner.

In northern Spain, in the cave of El Sidrón, scientists scraped the hardened plaque off Neanderthal teeth. Plaque traps tiny pieces of everything you eat. And for one group of Neanderthals, they found almost no meat at all. Instead they found mushrooms. Pine nuts. Moss. A cave full of the "meat-eating cavemen" from every cartoon, and some of them were basically eating like foragers in a forest.

No other animal stores food in a wrapper, runs a fat factory or bakes bread. Long before we were farmers, long before we were even us, we were already doing something no other creature on Earth does with its food. We were changing it. To understand why, you have to go back to the one thing this hungry little creature learned to do better than anything alive.

Picture an early human sitting by a fire, around 780,000 years ago, on the shore of a lake in what is now Israel. A place called Gesher Benot Ya'aqov. He has a fish. A big carp from the lake. And he doesn't eat it raw. He puts it near the fire.

We know this because scientists found thousands of fish teeth at that site. And when they studied them, they found the teeth had been heated to a gentle temperature, not burned in a fire, just warmed enough to cook the flesh. It's some of the oldest evidence anywhere of people cooking their food.

Here's why that matters more than it sounds. A raw diet is hard work. Chimpanzees spend around five or six hours a day just chewing. Their guts are huge because raw food is tough to break down, and a lot of the energy in it never gets used.

Cooking changes that. Heat breaks food down before it ever reaches your mouth. Starch turns soft and sweet. Meat gets tender. Toxins in some plants break down. Suddenly you get more energy out of every bite, and you need to spend way less time chewing.

Some scientists think that's what made us. That cooking let our guts shrink and our brains grow, because a brain is the hungriest organ in your body and cooked food could finally feed it. Others say our brains were already growing before cooking became common, and they argue about when fire really took over. They might never settle it.

But either way, it shows you the design underneath. We were never the animal that just ate what it found. We were the animal that changed food before eating it.

But fire is only the start. Because the most powerful trick wasn't heating food. It was grinding it.

In Italy, at a site called Bilancino, archaeologists found a flat stone and a smaller grinding stone, about 30,000 years old. They looked like nothing special. But when scientists checked the surface of the stones under a microscope, they found tiny grains of starch stuck in the cracks. Starch from the roots of cattails and ferns.

The same thing turned up in Russia and in the Czech Republic. And in a cave in southern Italy called Grotta Paglicci, on a grinding stone around 32,000 years old, they even found traces of wild oats that had been warmed before grinding, maybe to dry them out so they'd grind faster.

Think about what that means. In the middle of the Ice Age, people were digging up roots, drying them, grinding them into flour, and probably mixing that flour with water and cooking it into something like porridge or a flat cake. Flour is light. It keeps. You can carry it. A bag of flour is energy you can take with you across a frozen landscape.

And it wasn't just one or two plants. At a site in Israel called Ohalo II, people lived on the shore of the Sea of Galilee around 23,000 years ago. Their camp was burned and then flooded, and the mud preserved everything. Scientists identified the remains of around 140 different kinds of plants. Wild wheat. Wild barley. Fruits. Nuts. Seeds. Even what looks like one of the oldest grinding slabs used for grain.

One hundred and forty kinds of plants. Your weekly shop probably doesn't have that many.

But one person with a grinding stone is still just one person. What turned this into a real Ice Age menu was knowledge. And knowledge is the thing humans pass on better than anything alive.

Think about how much you'd need to know to eat like this. Which mushrooms feed you and which kill you. Which roots need to be cooked, and for how long, before they stop being poisonous. When the grain ripens. Where the shellfish live at low tide. How long a deer leg keeps before the marrow goes bad.

None of that is in your body. It's in your group. It was taught, by grandmothers and grandfathers, to children who watched and copied and remembered. A wolf knows how to hunt because it's born knowing. But a human child has to learn which berry is food and which berry is death. And a group of humans could remember thousands of things no single person could ever figure out alone.

That's the secret ingredient. The Ice Age diet wasn't one food. It was a library. And every generation added a page.

So now look at the "caveman diet" people sell today. The idea is simple. Eat like our ancestors. Lots of meat, no grains, no bread, nothing processed.

But you've just seen what our ancestors actually did. They ground wild grain into flour 30,000 years ago. They baked bread before farming existed. They boiled bones into grease, stored meat, and ate mushrooms, moss and pine nuts. And what they ate changed completely from place to place. People near the coast ate shellfish and seals. People in the forest ate nuts and roots. People on the frozen plains leaned on fat and meat. There was never one caveman diet. There were hundreds of them.

And the real rule underneath all of them was simple. Eat whatever you can find, and figure out a clever way to make it better.

So think about that fire again. The snow, the wind, the hunger. You thought you were waiting for a mammoth steak. But the person next to you is grinding roots into flour. Someone else is cracking open a stored bone for the marrow. A grandmother is pointing at a mushroom and telling a child, "That one, never that one."

You are carrying every piece of that right now. The gut that shrank because we cooked our food. The brain that grew because we fed it. The taste for fat and salt and sugar that kept your ancestors alive through the worst winters on Earth.

Next time you make toast, or boil pasta, or eat a sandwich, remember you're not eating something modern. You're following a recipe that's tens of thousands of years old. The first bread was baked by people who had never seen a farm. And the most human thing you'll do all day isn't something clever with your phone.

It's cooking dinner.

## Facts to check before recording

All from memory. Check them against the sources before narrating:

- **Shubayqa 1 bread:** charred flatbread remains ~14,400 years old, made from wild cereals (such as einkorn) and club-rush tubers, ~4,000 years before farming in the region (Arranz-Otaegui et al., 2018, *PNAS*).
- **Qesem Cave stored marrow:** fallow deer bones with skin-removal cut marks, ~400,000 to 200,000 years; experiments showed marrow kept for up to ~9 weeks (Blasco et al., 2019, *Science Advances*).
- **Neumark-Nord "fat factory":** Neanderthal bone-grease rendering from 170+ large mammals, ~125,000 years (Kindler et al., 2025, *Science Advances*). The 170+ figure is from memory, so check it.
- **El Sidrón diet:** dental calculus showing mushrooms, pine nuts and moss with little or no meat for one group (Weyrich et al., 2017, *Nature*).
- **Gesher Benot Ya'aqov cooked fish:** ~780,000 years, carp teeth heated at low temperatures (Zohar et al., 2022, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*).
- **Chimp chewing time:** about 5 to 6 hours a day is a widely quoted figure (Wrangham, *Catching Fire*, 2009).
- **Cooking hypothesis:** Richard Wrangham's idea that cooking drove brain growth and gut shrinkage. Debated, which the script says.
- **Grinding stones with starch:** Bilancino (Italy), Kostenki (Russia) and Pavlov (Czech Republic), ~30,000 years (Revedin et al., 2010, *PNAS*). Grotta Paglicci oats ~32,000 years (Mariotti Lippi et al., 2015, *PNAS*).
- **Ohalo II:** ~23,000 years, around 140 plant taxa, wild wheat and barley, grinding slab (Weiss et al., 2004, *PNAS*; Snir et al., 2015, *PLOS ONE*). "Around 140" is approximate.
- **Coastal diets:** Neanderthals at Gibraltar and Figueira Brava (Portugal) ate shellfish, seals and crabs. Not named in the script, so optional to cite.
