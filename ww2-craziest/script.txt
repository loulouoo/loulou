A bear that was officially enlisted in an army. A man who walked into battle with a sword and a bow. A spy who invented 27 people who didn't exist. A soldier who kept fighting for 29 years after the war ended. And a man who was hit by an atomic bomb, and then hit by another one three days later.

All of these are real. All of them happened in the Second World War.

World War Two was the biggest and deadliest war in history. But hidden inside it are stories so strange, so funny and so unbelievable that they sound made up. Today we're counting down the five craziest. And number one is so unlucky, and so lucky at the same time, that you'll have to hear it twice to believe it.

Let's start.

Number 5. The bear who joined the army.

It's 1942, and a group of Polish soldiers is travelling through Iran. They've had a horrible few years. Many of them had been prisoners in Soviet camps, and now they've been released to fight against Germany.

Near a mountain road, they meet a young boy carrying a sack. Inside the sack is a tiny, orphaned brown bear cub. The soldiers feel sorry for it, and they buy it from him for some money, a bit of chocolate and a tin of meat.

They name the bear Wojtek. And Wojtek becomes one of the lads.

He drinks condensed milk out of a vodka bottle. When he's older, he drinks beer. He's given cigarettes, and he eats them. He wrestles with the soldiers, rides in the front of their trucks and sleeps in their tents. One night, the story goes, he even caught a thief sneaking into the camp's supply hut, who got the scare of his life.

Then there was a problem. In 1944, the unit had to sail to Italy on a British ship, and the rules said no pets and no animals allowed.

So the Polish soldiers came up with a brilliant solution. They made Wojtek a soldier. He was officially enlisted in the army, with a name, a rank and a serial number. He wasn't a pet anymore. He was Private Wojtek.

In Italy, his unit fought in the Battle of Monte Cassino, one of the hardest battles of the war. And according to the soldiers who were there, Wojtek watched them carrying heavy boxes of artillery shells, and started copying them, carrying boxes himself.

After that, the unit's official badge became a picture of a bear carrying an artillery shell.

When the war ended, Wojtek moved to Scotland with his soldiers, and he spent the rest of his life at Edinburgh Zoo. And for years, old Polish veterans would come to visit him, call out to him in Polish, and throw him cigarettes over the fence.

He was a bear. But to them, he was a comrade.

Number 4. The man who brought a sword to a gun fight.

Most soldiers in World War Two carried a rifle. John Churchill carried a longbow, a set of bagpipes and a Scottish sword.

His friends called him Mad Jack, and it's easy to see why.

Mad Jack believed that any officer who went into battle without his sword was "improperly dressed". He had been an archer before the war. He had even appeared in a film, shooting arrows.

In 1940, as the Germans swept into France, Mad Jack's unit was waiting to ambush a German patrol. The signal to attack was Mad Jack shooting an arrow. And he did. It's said to be the last time a soldier was killed by an arrow in a modern war.

But he wasn't finished. In Italy in 1943, he went on a night raid with just one other soldier. Armed mostly with his sword, he crept up on a German position, and he came back with prisoners. About 42 of them. He walked them back to the British lines, still carrying his sword.

Later in the war, he was captured and sent to a prison camp in Germany. So he escaped. He was caught. So he escaped again.

And when the war finally ended, you'd think Mad Jack would be relieved. He wasn't. He's reported to have said: "If it wasn't for those damn Yanks, we could have kept the war going another 10 years!"

Number 3. The spy who invented 27 fake spies.

Juan Pujol García was a Spaniard who had run a chicken farm. He hated Hitler. He decided he wanted to help Britain win the war.

So he walked into the British embassy and offered to be a spy. The British said no.

He tried again. They said no again.

So Juan came up with a crazy plan. If the British wouldn't hire him, he'd get the Germans to hire him instead, and then trick them.

He told the Germans he was a loyal supporter who could travel to Britain and spy for them. They believed him. But he never actually went to Britain. He went to Lisbon in Portugal, and from there he made everything up. He used a tourist guidebook, maps and old magazines to write fake reports about Britain.

Some of his reports were ridiculous. He once told the Germans that the dockworkers in Glasgow "would do anything for a litre of wine". Anyone who had been to Glasgow would have laughed. But the Germans didn't notice.

Then Juan went further. He told the Germans he'd recruited other spies. Then more. Then more. In the end, he had invented 27 fake agents, each with a name, a personality and a back story. One of them was even "killed" off, and the Germans paid a pension to his made-up widow.

Finally, the British realised who this man was and what he'd done. They brought him to London and gave him the code name Garbo, after the famous actress, because he was the best actor in the world.

His biggest role came in 1944. The Allies were about to invade France, at Normandy. Garbo's job was to convince Hitler that Normandy was only a trick, and that the real invasion would come somewhere else, near Calais.

And it worked. Even after the D-Day landings had started, the Germans kept powerful forces waiting near Calais for an attack that was never coming.

Here's the best part. The Germans were so grateful to their star spy that they gave him the Iron Cross, one of their medals. And the British gave him the MBE. He's one of the only people to get medals from both sides of the war.

After the war, he faked his own death and disappeared. People only found out he was still alive decades later.

Number 2. The soldier who wouldn't stop fighting.

In December 1944, a young Japanese officer named Hiroo Onoda was sent to a small island in the Philippines called Lubang. His orders were to fight on, and never to surrender. Never.

A few months later, the Americans landed and took the island. Onoda and a few other soldiers ran into the jungle and hid.

Then, in 1945, the war ended.

Leaflets fell from the sky saying Japan had surrendered. Onoda thought they were fake. Newspapers and letters from his family were left in the jungle for him. He thought they were enemy tricks. Search parties came to find him. He hid from them.

So he stayed in the jungle. For years. He lived on bananas, coconuts and food taken from local farms. His companions were killed or left, one by one, until he was completely alone. And the story has a dark side too. Onoda and his men were still fighting, and over those years, people on the island were killed.

Then in 1974, a young Japanese traveller named Norio Suzuki decided to go and find him. He reportedly said he was going to look for three things: Lieutenant Onoda, a panda, and the Abominable Snowman, in that order.

Amazingly, he found Onoda. They talked and even took photos together. But Onoda still refused to give up. He would only stop if his commanding officer ordered him to.

So Japan found his old commander, who by then was working in a bookshop. He flew out to the island, walked into the jungle, and read Onoda his orders. The war was over. He could stop.

Onoda had been fighting for 29 years. He finally handed over his sword, still wearing his old uniform.

Number 1. The man who survived both atomic bombs.

In the summer of 1945, Tsutomu Yamaguchi was 29 years old. He was an engineer who designed ships for the company Mitsubishi. He lived in the city of Nagasaki, but he'd been sent to work in Hiroshima for three months.

On the morning of the 6th of August, it was his last day in Hiroshima. He was finally going home.

At 8:15, he was walking to work when he saw a plane in the sky, and then a flash. The first atomic bomb ever used in war had exploded about 3 kilometres away.

The blast threw him to the ground. His face and arms were badly burned. His eardrums burst. Most of the city around him was destroyed.

He spent the night in a shelter. And the next day, burned and bandaged, he did the only thing he could think of. He got on a train and went home. To Nagasaki.

On the 9th of August, he went back to his office, covered in bandages. He started telling his boss about the giant explosion in Hiroshima, and how one bomb had destroyed a whole city.

His boss didn't believe him. He said it was impossible for one bomb to do that.

And at that exact moment, the room filled with a white flash.

The second atomic bomb had exploded over Nagasaki. Again, Yamaguchi was about 3 kilometres away. Again, he survived.

He went on to live for another 65 years. He had children, worked as a teacher and translator, and in his later years he travelled and spoke out against nuclear weapons, so that no one would ever have to go through what he did. Japan officially recognised him as a survivor of both bombings, and he died in 2010 at the age of 93.

Was he the unluckiest man in history, or the luckiest? Maybe both.

So there you have it. A bear soldier. A man with a sword. A spy who made up a whole army of spies. A soldier who fought for 29 years. And a man who survived both atomic bombs.

What these stories show is that war isn't only about numbers and battles. It's about people, and people are strange, brave, funny, stubborn and amazing.
